ITHACA, N.Y. — An ambitious but divisive plan has emerged for the long-term future of the Cayuga Power Plant in Lansing, which has remained murky in the six years since its last days as a coal-fired electric generating station. If the plan comes to fruition, the plant could soon take on a new life as a high-powered data center, amid burgeoning investment into the Artificial Intelligence (AI) industry by tech industry giants such as Google and Meta. Maryland-based technology company TeraWulf Inc. wants to use the infrastructure in the now-shuttered power plant to build a data center. Such facilities contain banks of energy-intensive computers necessary for high-performance computing operations such as bitcoin mining and artificial intelligence. TeraWulf has secured an 80-year lease that calls for a data center at the Cayuga Drive site which could, at its max capacity, consume enough electricity in a year to power roughly 500,000 homes in New York, according to analysis of average annual energy consumption data from the federal government and New York State Energy and Gas (NYSEG). The project will be developed alongside an adjacent solar farm and a battery storage system. The proposal, formally announced in an Aug. 14 press release , has prompted renewed criticism from environmental advocates and hesitation from some local officials and Lansing residents. It’s not the first time TeraWulf — or one of its affiliate companies — has attempted a similar project. In 2022, a TeraWulf affiliate proposed a plan to use infrastructure at the Milliken Station facility to build a data center for cryptocurrency mining operations. The plan ultimately did not come to fruition and the facility has remained idle since the power plant stopped operating in 2019. The new data center lease grants TeraWulf exclusive access to develop up to 400 MW of digital infrastructure capacity— more than double the energy needs of the plan proposed in 2022, which had a proposed capacity of 150 MW . The company has undertaken similar plans at other sites Upstate. TeraWulf already operates a massive, multi-phase high-powered computing facility in the town of Somerset, an hour north of Buffalo. The facility, which the company calls its “Lake Mariner Campus,” was also originally a power plant. Infrastructure in electrical plants like Milliken Station translate well to the needs of a data center — high-performance computing consumes a lot of electricity and generates a lot of exhaust heat, which must be dissipated. The plant has the high capacity electrical infrastructure necessary to transfer large amounts of power between the plant and the grid. Additionally, the plant’s lake water intake infrastructure, once used in power generation, may be used to keep the facility’s computers from overheating. “A deal with himself” The data center plan has been years in the making and involves a complex tangle of TeraWulf subsidiaries and affiliate companies, all of which are under the ownership or management of its CEO, Paul Prager. In 2016, a TeraWulf affiliate, Riesling Power LLC, acquired the plant and the surrounding 183 acres. The plant is owned by Riesling subsidiary Cayuga Operating Company LLC, which continued to operate the facility as a coal fired power plant until 2019, when it was decommissioned . Riesling’s parent company is Heorot Power Holdings LLC, which is owned by TeraWulf CEO Paul Prager. The companies are all managed by BeoWulf, which itself is owned by TeraWulf. Anna Kelles, who represents Tompkins County in the New York State Assembly, has been an outspoken critic of such projects. In 2022, Kelles spearheaded a landmark bill that placed a temporary moratorium on companies looking to convert fossil fuel power plants into cryptocurrency mining centers. Kelles said she has concerns about TeraWulf’s complex ownership structure. “[Prager] did a deal with himself,” Kelles said. TeraWulf, which is a publicly traded company and thus subject to additional federal regulations, operates in part through affiliated limited liability corporations (LLCs), which do not have to meet those regulations. “This is how the wealthy play. This is how the rich play,” Kelles said. “They build a bunch of mega companies. They know how to manipulate the system.” TeraWulf did not respond to multiple requests for comment. “A huge demand on the system: 24/7, never relenting” Though the company will not resume burning coal to generate power for the data center, environmentalists and local officials have concerns. Most of the electricity needed to keep the computers running will come from the local electrical grid. Unlike the average home, where energy usage fluctuates through the day and seasons, data centers require power at all times. On top of regular infrastructure, the “always on” nature of such facilities necessitates redundancy in the form of additional generators, batteries and internet connections. In order to build out the full infrastructure plan, the company must still seek approval and permits from the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation and the Town of Lansing Planning Board. While TeraWulf had not yet submitted official site plans, representatives from its parent company BeoWulf met with town officials late last year to present their vision for the property, which includes the data center itself, an adjacent solar farm and a battery array to store electricity. But Town of Lansing Deputy Supervisor Joe Wetmore said there’s still a lot of questions. “I’ve got a lot of vague things that have been said, but without a site plan, it’s really hard to say, ‘Oh, it’s going to have this impact,’” Wetmore said. Wetmore said he was told by the plant operator that while a cryptocurrency mine can be turned on and off, AI data centers cannot. “That’s an issue because that’s going to put a huge demand on the system. 24/7, never relenting,” Wetmore said. In recent years, many cryptocurrency mining operations have shifted their focus to meeting the computing needs of the rising AI market — an industry that some see as relatively stable compared to cryptocurrency. In its press release, the company claims that by 2026, 138 MW of the total 400 MW allocated for the data center would come from “predominantly zero-carbon” sources. The release claims that nearly 90% of the electricity generated in upstate New York comes from zero-carbon sources. In reality, according to data from NYSEG, which owns the local grid that would supply the data center, only around 35% of their total energy mix is from zero-carbon sources .
