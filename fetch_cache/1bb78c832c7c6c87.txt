But that peaceful waterfall, and the groundwater that feeds it, could soon be threatened by a neighbor unlike any this rural Twiggs County community has ever seen. A proposed $5 billion data center on nearly 300 acres, located down the road from Faulk's property, has sparked fierce opposition from environmental advocates and longtime residents who fear the massive facility will drain their wells and destroy critical wildlife habitat. "My third great grandfather came here in 1806," Faulk said, standing near the historic family homestead. "This is the birthplace of all the Faulks of Georgia." The Weyerhaeuser NR Company project would rezone 291.5 acres from agricultural to general industrial use on Highway 129 in Dry Branch. But the proposal sits on a larger 890-acre tract, raising concerns about future expansion that could fundamentally alter this quiet corner of Central Georgia. Wells Could Cost $15,000 to Replace Faulk's biggest fear centers on his water supply. Like most people in the area, he relies on a private well that taps into the same groundwater system the data center would need for cooling its massive servers. "If my well goes dry, who's gonna replace that?" Faulk said. "That's a big expense to everyone who lives here." Wells in the area cost approximately $15,000 to replace, and soil conditions make drilling new ones difficult in some locations. Data centers typically require millions of gallons of water daily for cooling systems, leading residents to worry about competition for the finite groundwater supply. "We're very worried," said Nancy Lubeck, who lives about a mile from the proposed site. "From what we understand, they will be drilling wells. There are a lot of unanswered questions that nobody seems to have a definite answer to." Bears' Last Stand The environmental concerns extend far beyond individual property lines. Just miles from the proposed data center lies the 12,400-acre Oaky Woods wilderness area, home to Middle Georgia's entire population of black bears. John Trussell, a Georgia Master Naturalist who has studied the region's wildlife for years, says the bear population numbers only about 300 animals — making it extremely vulnerable to habitat loss. "Every time you take wildlife habitat and destroy it, those animals, you know, are basically they're taking away their homes," Trussell said. The area has already suffered significant environmental impact from a 2,000-acre solar farm approved by Twiggs County commissioners two years ago. Trussell worries the data center represents another step toward industrializing what was once a pristine wildlife corridor. "It's been particularly harmful to our bear population," Trussell said. "It's a very small, fragile population we have to carefully monitor, and this increases that damage." National Security Questions The proposed facility's proximity to Robins Air Force Base has raised additional concerns about national security implications. The data center would sit within what Trussell calls the "REPI zone," an area identified by the Air Force as influential to the base's future operations. "We need to be concerned and asking questions about data centers next to our military installations and how they're going to be impacted and who they're gonna be controlled by," Trussell said. Commission Vote Looms County Commission Chairman Ken Fowler acknowledged in a previous story that the project could "triple our tax base" but admitted he lacks detailed information about environmental impacts. "Now, what we do to the environment or how it affects the citizens of the county, I have no idea," Fowler said. "I don't have enough information really to talk about that." The Twiggs County Board of Commissioners will hold a public hearing on the proposal Thursday, Sept. 18, at 6 p.m. at the county courthouse. If approved by a majority of the five-member commission, the rezoning would clear a major hurdle for the project to proceed. "I just can't believe that the revenue is gonna outweigh the problems," Faulk said.
